## The dataset

The data comes from GBIF: every eBird record for India from 1800 to 2021, roughly 30 million rows and about 3GB on disk. The goal was to mine it for meaningful trends about threatened species, centered on one of my favorite IUCN-listed birds, Pallas's Fish Eagle (*Haliaeetus leucoryphus*).

</section>

<section>

## Fitting it in memory

Loaded with Pandas defaults, the dataframe ate over 2GB of RAM, too much to explore comfortably. The fix was simply choosing the right dtype for each column: 32-bit floats for coordinates and counts, small integers for day and month, and categoricals for the repeated string columns like state and species. That alone brought the footprint down to just over **770MB**.

```python
def set_dtypes(df):
    df['individualCount'] = df['individualCount'].astype('float32')
    df['decimalLatitude'] = df['decimalLatitude'].astype('float32')
    df['decimalLongitude'] = df['decimalLongitude'].astype('float32')
    df['day'] = df['day'].astype('int8')
    df['month'] = df['month'].astype('int8')
    df['year'] = df['year'].astype('int16')
    df['stateProvince'] = df['stateProvince'].astype('category')
    df['species'] = df['species'].astype('category')
    return df
```

<Callout label="The cheap win">
Categoricals and right-sized numeric types cut the working set by nearly two thirds, with no loss of information. On a 30-million-row dataset, that is the difference between exploring interactively and waiting on every operation.
</Callout>

## Tracking the eagle

### Where it lives

The first cut was state-wise sightings. Assam leads by a wide margin, with Uttarakhand close behind, and everywhere else falling off sharply, the eagle is concentrated in just a couple of regions.

### When it shows up

Grouping by month makes the eagle's seasonality obvious: sightings climb through winter and fall to nearly zero in summer, confirming it as a winter visitor to India.

<Figure src="https://cdn.hashnode.com/res/hashnode/image/upload/v1730263178947/559bb0ac-cdee-4366-90f4-246a5ea980e5.png" alt="Sightings by month" caption="A clear winter-visitor signature." />

### Zooming into Assam

Narrowing to Assam, one wrinkle showed up: Kaziranga, Manas, and Nameri National Parks each split across many separately named ranges in the data. Merging those with a few regex passes was necessary before the locality picture made sense, and once merged, nearly all of Assam's sightings collapse onto Kaziranga National Park.

```python
merged_state = df_spc.query('stateProvince == "Assam"') \
    .groupby(df_spc['locality']
        .apply(lambda x: re.sub('.*kaziranga.*', 'Kaziranga NP', x, flags=re.IGNORECASE))
        .apply(lambda x: re.sub('.*nameri.*', 'Nameri NP', x, flags=re.IGNORECASE))
        .apply(lambda x: re.sub('.*manas.*', 'Manas NP', x, flags=re.IGNORECASE)))['individualCount'] \
    .sum()
```

### The trend over time

Year over year, sightings climb steadily from 2002, spike sharply around 2018, then ease back while staying well above the early baseline. That likely reflects a mix of more observers and possible range or population shifts, the two are hard to separate from sightings alone.

## The observer-effort confound

That last caveat matters, because eBird itself exploded in India over the same window. Plotting total yearly contributions shows minimal activity before 2014 and a steep climb from 2015 on.

<Figure src="https://cdn.hashnode.com/res/hashnode/image/upload/v1730264329366/95feb02f-3786-4d48-9ca4-3d74a930fd1e.png" alt="Yearly eBird contributions in India" caption="eBird participation in India surges after 2014." />

So a rising sighting count does not cleanly mean a rising population, more people are simply looking. Separating real population trends from observer effort is the genuinely hard part of this kind of analysis.

</section>

<section>

## On detecting decline

The pipeline here, filter to a species, then visualize across space, season, and time, generalizes to any bird in the dataset, which makes it a useful lens for threatened species. I also tried to automatically flag species in decline, and that turned out to be statistically thorny. Raw percentage change misfired badly on vagrants and species with too few sightings to be significant. Linear regression and the Mann-Kendall test were better but still flagged some vagrants. It is unfinished, but it pointed clearly at what a more reliable trend indicator would need to account for.

</section>
